Alright. Attention ethics. For a long time I've struggled with posting online. 

Online attention gives people two main, and overlapping, things. First, money, plain and 
simple. Having followers is an excellent marketing lever. It leads all sorts of partnerships.
Online attention also confers, social status, which correlates with viability as a sexual partner.

In a way, social media games are the same as the real world. Humans are motivated by money, 
and sex. Of course those two draws are deeply related, but that's out of scope of this essay.

What bugs me about the online posting is that, normal things you would say to friends, are
morally suspect online. And this introduces a sort of cognitive dissonance: what's wrong with posts
containingsimple sentences like "NYC rocks" accompanied by a picture of Central Park? 

This sort of statement is generally fine with a friend. You're just expressing an opinion. Your
friend, by hanging out with you, is implicitly signing up to have a conversation. 

But say you post "NYC rocks" on Twitter. If posted to a general audience, you're saying something
different. You're saying your opinion, specifically what cities are cool, matters. Implicit in 
your post is that you know what's going on in a cosmopolitan sense. 

I think the danger here lies in the dissonence between statements which make sense in person,
but when said online, suggest different things. And further, as most know, attention is the 
currency on social media, not being right. 

I still think there's a bit of a lag in terms of people's understanding (or maybe just my own, which
is why I'm writing this) of how deep the problem is. Attention is a scarce resource. If posting,
primarily for attention, a person is gunning to take a scarce resource from other people. Logging on 
to general social media platforms means you're signing up to consume content with set of incentives 
which are very different from hanging out with people. 

The problem is that social media isn't "a scam." I've used Twitter DMs to find jobs I've held for 
years. It's extremely powerful to be able to put out feelers about work, about an idea you're
tinkering with, to an undefined audience. Sometimes you don't know exactly where to look to find
collaborators, feedback, partners. So it's great to shoot shots on social media. 

What I'm getting at is context of written/typed language is as important as the words themselves. 
We all sense this implicitly. A text to a friend is not a "post" to the world. But if you never post
online, you miss out on the potential upside of finding collaborators, friends, or opportunities,
which is one of the great powers of the internet.  

In terms of solutions to this problem, I think we could expand modalities of "posting." You can
post to close friends on Instagram, but that limits serendipitious upside. So does posting on 
private modes on major social media sites. You can post in Slacks and Discords, but if someone doesn't 
happen to see the message, it's quickly subsumed. 

In a way, custom forums, and small Reddits, are the gold standard of online communication. A friend
who runs a kiwi farm referred me to The Tropical Fruit Forum, when I brought up this line of thinking.
People are trying to get things done on the Tropical Fruit Forum. Same with forums for specific cars.
Maybe clout exists on those sites too, but it's localized, focused on a specific subcommunity which
has an implicit set of interests.

And attention seeking posts, don't seem to thrive in those forums. Why is that? I think it's for 
the same reason that local businesses can cater to their neighborhood in a way that chains 
can't: local businesses have to put in the work a head of time to understand their customers. This 
transfers over to posting online: the environment in which you post matters as much as the words. It's
about placement. And I think it's dangerous to exist in these generalized social media platforms. 
There's too much downside: when posters just have to attract enough people globally, they will say
anything in order to do so. They don't care about negative externalities. 

An example of this would be 9 year-old boys who listen to Andrew Tate describe women as 
"sub-sentient." This attracts attention on generalized platforms, where as on your local birding 
forum, there simply aren't enough people to get "rage-baited" by purposely inflammatory posts.

Again, though, there is still promise in global platforms. 

Not to make this an advertisement, but I have worked with a friend on a project exploring technical
solutions to the problem above, which I would essentially call: can we have the upside of global 
communication without the downside of attention-seeking posts which steal time, a person's, 
ultimately, most valuable resource.

think it's net negative. You're posting in order to be read, listened to, engaged with. 
But how often do people post what they would actually want to read, as opposed to 
following a vague, or not-so-vague, impulse to subtly (or not) signal status?

My point is though, "content creator" businesses are, on balance, unethical. Again,
no shit, the world is unethical. Humans kill each other. But I think what's insidious
here is that the content creator's output is "information." It's "saying things," 
creating videos. Content creator businesses don't work unless they consume other 
people's time, which is arguably, their most important resource. 

And that implicit fact about content creators, should actually come with a warning, 
like a food label. I'm not sure how to design that. But something like a ratings agency,
the Moody's of content creators, asigning an independent assessment of an account's 
output. You could potentially layer it on your social media platform somehow, so you
could choose what you consume. "Following" tabs on Twitter et al. do this to some degree,
but it would be cool to get the discovery of new accounts, but through a lens you could
trust. Obviously that's a thorny issue and talk is cheap there.
