Alright. Attention ethics. For a long time I've struggled with posting online. 

Online attention gives people two main, and overlapping, things. First, money. Having followers is
a personal marketing lever. Online attention also confers a vaporous thing, "social status,"
which correlates somewhat with perceived viability as a sexual partner.

In a way, online attention isn't so different than offline attention.

 Money and sex are key human

The strange thing about online posting is that, normal things in person mean something else
online. A simple post like "good morning" on Twitter means something very different than someone
saying that in person.

More so than that, "good morning" in text, is qualitatively identical no matter who says it. 
But who says it, and when, changes the meaning of "good morning." A famous actor tweeting "good
morning" the day after a DUI, means a very different thing than a politician tweeting "good morning"
after winning an election. A scandal broke by the Wall Street Journal means a different thing than 
the exact same text on an unknown Substack. 

Extrapolating from this means that there is "metadata" attached to text. The information matters,
but so does the context. As AI uncaps supply of digital information, it's possible that the 
context will begin to gain in power relative to the information. The information is the easy part,
the hard part is the metadata: who says it, and other visual aspects like font choice as well.  

Of course, there's metadata in-person too. Clothes, looks, tone of voice. There are infinite
ways to say "hi." That said, the internet has different metadata than in-person. In a way, the 
internet emphasizes more factual metadata. Height, follower count, university attended. These 
are facts, not vibes.

Of course, I'm focused on the written word, likely because of my time on Twitter. Instagram's metadata
likely actually rewards factual metadata less, with looks being the most important. 

What am I getting at? It could just be infantile frustration with seeing the power of the web, but
not wanting to compete for attention, as I think, the internet flattens everything into "content," 
into metadata games rather than being about the thing itself. 

I think what I'm getting at is that the internet, and specifically social media, as currently constructed,
is geared towards metadata, rather than the information itself. Having strong metadata is how you 
get attention online. What you say matters, but your metadata matters potentially more. 

These means that, the incentives are clear: people should optimize for metadata in order for their posts 
to have weight, meaning that they will get attention, the currency of the web. In some ways, yes, it's
frustrating  that people spend so much time optimizing for "metadata" rather than "getting 
things done."

I still think there's a bit of a lag in terms of people's understanding (or maybe just my own, which
is why I'm writing this) of how deep the problem is. Attention is a scarce resource. If posting,
primarily for attention, a person is gunning to take a scarce resource from other people. Logging on 
to general social media platforms means you're signing up to consume content with set of incentives 
which are very different from hanging out with people.

What I'm getting at is context of written/typed language is as important as the words themselves. 
We all sense this implicitly. A text to a friend is not a "post" to the world. But if you never post
online, you miss out on the potential upside of finding collaborators, friends, or opportunities,
which is one of the great powers of the internet.  

In terms of solutions to this problem, I think we could expand modalities of "posting." You can
post to close friends on Instagram, but that limits serendipitious upside. So does posting on 
private modes on major social media sites. You can post in Slacks and Discords, but if someone doesn't 
happen to see the message, it's quickly subsumed.

People are trying to get things done on the Tropical Fruit Forum. Same with forums for specific cars.
Maybe clout exists on those sites too, but it's localized, focused on a specific subcommunity which

And attention seeking posts, don't seem to thrive in those forums. Why is that? I think it's for 
the same reason that local businesses can cater to their neighborhood in a way that chains 
can't: local businesses have to put in the work a head of time to understand their customers. This 
transfers over to posting online: the environment in which you post matters as much as the words. It's
about placement. And I think it's dangerous to exist in these generalized social media platforms. 
There's too much downside: when posters just have to attract enough people globally, they will say
anything in order to do so. They don't care about negative externalities. 

An example of this would be 9 year-old boys who listen to Andrew Tate describe women as 
"sub-sentient." This attracts attention on generalized platforms, where as on your local birding 
forum, there simply aren't enough people to get "rage-baited" by purposely inflammatory posts.

Again, though, there is still promise in global platforms.
